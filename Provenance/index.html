01 — Editorial

Photorealism was the wrong finish line.

For three years, image-generation benchmarks have asked one question: does it look real? That question is quietly being answered. It's time to ask a harder one.

Every major image model released in the last eighteen months has closed the gap on anatomy, lighting, and skin texture. Six-fingered hands are a punchline from an earlier era. The failures that once defined an evaluation — warped faces, impossible shadows, garbled text — have become rare enough that flagging them barely counts as analysis anymore.

That convergence is real, and it changes what a benchmark is for. If every frontier model can render a photorealistic human being, photorealism stops being a differentiator and starts being a baseline. The interesting question moves one layer deeper: when a model renders a family in Punjab versus a family in Uttar Pradesh, does it actually know the difference — or does it reach for the same generic vocabulary of "Indian-ness" regardless of which state it was asked for?

We built a small, deliberately narrow benchmark to test exactly that. Three models. Ten states. One identical prompt per state, with no reference image to lean on. We then asked people — blind to which model made which image — to tell us not whether the picture was beautiful, but whether it was true.

What we found is not a story about one model beating another. It's a story about where the frontier has actually moved.

Why this matters A model that can't distinguish a Gujarati household from a Rajasthani one isn't just aesthetically generic — it's unreliable for education, media, and any product that claims to represent a culture rather than caricature it.
What we didn't test Reference-image conditioning, video, sub-regional or district-level detail, and historical accuracy are all out of scope for this pass. See Future Work.
A note on scale This is a pilot, not a census: 8 participants, 79 evaluations, one prompt per state. We treat every finding here as a strong signal worth investigating further — not a settled verdict.

Field Notes — Read Before You Read the Charts
  • Two participants' response sets are identical. Every one of Daksh's and Sushant's 40 individual answers (10 states × 4 questions) matches exactly. We can't determine from the data whether this is a genuine coincidence of independent taste or a duplicated submission. Per the research team's direction, both are retained and counted as independent contributors throughout this report — we flag it here so the reader can weigh it accordingly.
  • One participant voted a single model per state. Amit selected the same letter across all four questions for 9 of 10 states, rather than discriminating between "most realistic" and "best follows prompt" separately. His votes are counted as written, but read as one composite preference per state rather than four independent signals.
  • State identification was 96% accurate — 76 of 79 responses. All three misidentifications came from the same participant (Amit): Uttar Pradesh called Haryana, Kerala called Andhra Pradesh, and Jammu & Kashmir shortened to Kashmir.

05 — Case Study

Uttar Pradesh: winning by not losing.

The most decisive result in the dataset is also the most misleading one, if you read it too quickly.

78%
Consensus share for GPT Image 1 on Uttar Pradesh — tied for the highest of any state
1 / 3
Misidentifications in the entire study happened on this one state

Uttar Pradesh produced the single most one-sided result of the investigation: GPT Image 1 won every prompt-following vote (8 of 8) and nearly every other category. By the numbers, it looks like the state where localization worked best.

Read the justifications, and a different story appears.

Mughees, Participant 03

"Others had diya-on-table error."

Abhigyan, Participant 06

"Generic North India."

GPT's image won not because participants believed it was distinctly, unmistakably Uttar Pradesh — it won because the two competing models made more visible mistakes, like a diya positioned incorrectly above a dinner table. And even the winning image wasn't safe: one participant, evaluating what the group had just rated the state's best image, misidentified it as Haryana — a neighboring state with an overlapping but distinct visual identity.

This is the gap between a relative win and absolute cultural fidelity, and it's the clearest evidence in this study that consensus alone can overstate how well a model actually understands a place. Compare this to Tamil Nadu, which had almost identical consensus (78.2%) but zero misidentifications and comments citing accurate script and festival detail — a win for the right reasons, not just the fewest wrong ones.

06 — Failure Mode Investigation

Five ways a culturally competent image still goes wrong.

Documented by the research team during image review, and corroborated where possible by participant justifications above.

✕
Generic North Indian Representation

Uttar Pradesh's household resembled a composite of North Indian visual tropes rather than anything specific to UP — the failure this study's case study is built around.

Localization Failure
✕
Contextual Hallucination

A Punjabi household scene included Gandhi's spinning wheel (charkha) — a nationally recognizable symbol, but not one tied to Punjabi domestic life specifically. The model reached for "recognizably Indian" instead of "recognizably Punjabi."

Contextual Error
✕
Decoration Bias

Marigold garlands, one of the most common AI shorthand cues for "Indian festival," appeared in states where they aren't part of the typical decorative vocabulary — a texture the model over-generalized from wherever it appears most often in training data.

Cultural Leakage
✕
Common-Sense Error

A Uttar Pradesh scene placed a lit diya directly above a dinner table — the exact error one participant cited by name as the reason a competing model lost the state.

Common Sense
✕
Misaligned Festival Greeting

On-image festival text didn't fully match the visual context surrounding it — the greeting and the scene told two slightly different stories about what was being celebrated.

Contextual Error

The unexpected discovery: language did more work than food.

Going in, we expected regional cuisine to be the strongest cultural signal. It wasn't. Most models rendered regionally correct food across nearly every state, so it stopped being a useful signal for telling models apart — participants barely mentioned it. What they mentioned constantly was on-image script: Gurmukhi in Punjab, Gujarati lettering with Navratri, Devanagari for Diwali, Malayalam for Onam, Tamil for Pongal. When a model rendered real, correctly-scripted regional text, participants trusted the whole image more — even though script accuracy says nothing about anatomy or lighting. We'd flag this as an interesting emergent cue, not a deliberate design win: none of the three models were built to optimize for legible regional typography, and we don't yet know how reliable it is outside this sample.

07 — Original Framework

The Cultural Localization Pyramid.

A proposed lens for reading any cultural-image benchmark — including this one. Not a benchmark output; an interpretive frame the research team is proposing for how to think about results like these.

Every model in this study cleared the bottom tier almost perfectly. The separation between them opened up in the middle and top tiers — and that's where we think future benchmarks, including the objective layer of this one, should spend their attention.

Reasoning
Does the model understand why an element belongs — coherently composing correct cultural knowledge into a scene, not just placing recognizable objects near each other?
Widest divergence between models
Localization
Can the model tell this state apart from its neighbors — Uttar Pradesh from Haryana, Kerala from Andhra Pradesh — rather than defaulting to a regional generic?
Where this study found its clearest failures
Recognition
Can the model render "an Indian family," "a festival," "traditional dress" — legibly, photorealistically, without anatomical error?

08 — The Report

What we'd tell each lab, and what we'd tell ourselves.

Recommendations, limitations, and the next phase of this work.

GPT Image 1

Protect the edge, widen it

  • Strongest on prompt-following and educational suitability — investigate whether legible regional-script generation can be made a reliable, tested capability rather than an emergent side-effect.
  • Realism parity with Gemini means the next differentiator is depth of state-specific detail, not surface polish.
Gemini 3.1

The improvement is real — extend it

  • The single largest generational jump in this study (+41 points over Gemini 2.5). Whatever training or architecture change drove that should be identified and doubled down on.
  • Led "Best Represents" outright — worth understanding why it's trusted for authenticity even where it trails on prompt precision.
Gemini 2.5

Still competitive on realism alone

  • Tied for the realism lead despite trailing on every localization-adjacent metric — confirms photorealism and cultural accuracy are separable capabilities that need to be evaluated separately.
  • Weakest on prompt-following (17 of 79) — the clearest, most actionable gap to close.
Small Panel

8 participants and 79 evaluations is enough to surface directional signal, not enough to treat any single-state result as statistically settled.

One Prompt, One Draw

Each model generated one image per state. We don't know how much of any result reflects a single generation's luck versus the model's typical behavior.

Subjective Scoring

"Best represents" and "most realistic" were left to participant judgment without a shared, written rubric — the Objective Benchmark instrument in Section 02 exists specifically to address this in the next phase.

Language-Cue Confound

If legible regional script drove trust as strongly as Section 06 suggests, participants may have been partly evaluating typography rendering rather than cultural accuracy per se. Worth isolating in a follow-up.

Two Identical Response Sets

Flagged in Section 02 — Daksh's and Sushant's full response sets match exactly. Retained per the research team's decision, but any re-analysis should test sensitivity to this pair.

The first generation of AI learned to create images that looked real. The next generation must learn to create images that are culturally trustworthy. As photorealism becomes commonplace, the true measure of intelligence will no longer be visual fidelity, but the ability to retrieve, localize, and coherently compose cultural knowledge into authentic human experiences.
This benchmark is an attempt to measure that next frontier.
